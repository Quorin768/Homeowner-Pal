Utility vs homeowner, who owns what

One of the most useful things a homeowner can know: where the utility's responsibility ends and yours begins. The short version, the power company owns up to the meter, and you own everything from the meter into the house.

Utility's wires → Your wires

→

Utility owns Homeowner owns
  • Utility owns: transformer, service drop (overhead) or service lateral (underground), and the meter itself.
  • You own: the weatherhead, mast, meter base/can, main panel, every breaker, and every wire in your walls.
  • Who to call: tree on the service drop, no power on the whole street, meter pulled or tampered with → utility. Sparks at an outlet, breaker won't reset, panel buzzing, meter base damaged → licensed electrician. Smoke, flames, or burning smell you can't locate → 911 first, electrician second.

Demarcation varies slightly by utility, some draw the line at the weatherhead, some at the line side of the meter. Either way, the meter base, mast, and panel are always yours.

Inside your main panel

The panel is the heart of the home's electrical system. Power comes in through the main breaker, splits across two hot bus bars, and feeds individual breakers that protect each circuit. Neutrals return on the neutral bar; ground wires land on the ground bar.

MAIN — Main breaker
Hot bus barsBranch breakers
Neutral barGround bar
Bonding screw (only at MAIN, never sub-panel)
  • Bonding screw: the green screw that ties neutral to ground. It belongs only in the main panel (or main disconnect). In sub-panels, neutrals and grounds must be on separate, isolated bars.
  • Sub-panels need 4 wires: two hots, an insulated neutral, and a separate ground. Three-wire sub-feeds are an old-code holdover and are no longer allowed for new installs.
  • Double-tapping: putting two wires under one breaker terminal is not allowed on most breakers and is one of the most common inspection callouts.

Ampacity chart, what size wire for what breaker

Ampacity is the maximum current a wire can safely carry. Undersized wire overheats and starts fires; a breaker only protects the wire if the wire is sized correctly. Use this chart as a quick reference.

Based on NEC Table 310.16. Use the 60°C column for standard NM-B (Romex). 75°C applies to most THHN/THWN in conduit. Derating for ambient temp and conductor bundling still applies, when in doubt, size up.

Wire ampacity and breaker size are not always the same. Most residential 12 AWG branch circuits are protected by a 20 A breaker even though the conductor's ampacity may be higher depending on insulation type and installation conditions.

Rule of thumb for residential: 14 AWG = 15 A, 12 AWG = 20 A, 10 AWG = 30 A. Never put a larger breaker on a smaller wire.

Wire color conventions

  • Black, red, blue: hot (ungrounded) conductors, these are the wires that carry the actual voltage from the breaker to your lights and outlets. Think of them as the "live" wires that push electricity through the circuit. 120 V circuits typically use black; 240 V circuits use black + red.
  • Black + white on a 240 V breaker? Some 240 V loads, like a straight-resistive water heater or the compressor side of an A/C, don't need a neutral. In those cases the white wire is re-tasked as a second hot (both black and white land on the breaker lugs). No neutral is present in the disconnect box.
  • White: neutral. Exception: in switch loops fed by 2-wire cable, the white is re-marked black or red and used as a hot, common in older work, no longer allowed for new switch loops without a neutral.
  • Green or bare copper: equipment ground. Never used as a current-carrying conductor.
  • Gray: neutral in some commercial/industrial settings, uncommon in homes.

Single-pole switch wiring (toggle / Decora)

A single-pole switch is the most common device in a house: one switch, one light. All it does is break the hot wire. Power comes in on one brass screw, and when you flip the switch it leaves out the other brass screw on its way to the fixture, that outgoing wire is the switch leg (sometimes called the switched hot). The neutral never touches a standard switch; it gets spliced through the box and runs straight to the light.

Before you touch anything: turn off the breaker, then prove it's dead with a non-contact tester or meter at the device, not just at the switch plate. Some boxes contain a second circuit. If you're not comfortable confirming it's dead, stop and call a licensed electrician.
Single-pole toggle switch, front view, plate removed
OFF
15A 120V AC ONLY
Brass screw (top): Line hot in, black from the breaker
Brass screw (bottom): Switch leg out, black to the light
Green ground screw: lower left of the strap, bare/green wire lands here

Either brass screw can take either wire, a single pole isn't polarized. No neutral on the switch, white wires splice together in the box and pass through (smart switches are the exception). Toggle vs Decora: a Decora paddle has the same two brass screws and same ground screw.

Step by step
  1. Kill the breaker and verify dead at the box.
  2. Identify the wires. With power on (before you shut it off) or with a tester, find which black wire is the constant hot coming from the panel. The other black is the switch leg to the light.
  3. Strip about 3/4" of insulation. Bend a hook with your linesman or needle-nose pliers.
  4. Hook each hot wire clockwise around a brass screw so tightening pulls the loop closed, and snug it down firmly. One wire per screw, never two.
  5. Land the bare/green ground on the green screw. If the box is metal, the box gets bonded too, usually with a pigtail off the same ground splice.
  6. Splice the white neutrals together with a wire connector and tuck them back. They pass through; they do not land on the switch.
  7. Fold the wires accordion-style, seat the device square in the box, screw it in, then plate on, breaker on, and test.
Toggle vs Decora vs smart
  • Toggle (the classic flip lever) and Decora (the wide rocker/paddle) are wired identically. Two brass screws, one green screw. The only real difference is the trim style and the plate it takes. Don't overthink swapping one for the other.
  • Backstabs vs screws: many switches have push-in holes on the back. Terminal screws hold better and fail less over time, use the screws.
  • Smart switches and dimmers usually need a neutral in the box to power their electronics. If your box only has a two-wire cable ("switch loop," common in older homes), you either need a no-neutral-rated device or new cable pulled.
Common mistakes we get called out for
  • White wire used as a hot in an old switch loop and never re-marked. It's legal in existing work only if it's re-identified with black tape or marker, and it's a real shock hazard for the next person if it isn't.
  • No ground landed on the device when the box has one available.
  • Hot and switch leg swapped, the light will still work on a single-pole switch, but if the box feeds anything else downstream you can end up switching more than you intended.
  • Box fill, cramming a smart switch plus five splices into a shallow box. Box volume is a code limit, not a suggestion.
  • Buzzing, warm switch plates, or a switch that has to be "held just right" to work means the device or a connection is failing. Replace it, or get a licensed electrician on it.

Standard duplex receptacle wiring (and Decora-style)

A duplex receptacle is two outlets in one device. Wiring it is about getting three things on the right screws: hot to brass, neutral to silver, and ground to green. Brass is the darker screw, silver is the lighter one, easy way to remember it: black wire to brass, white wire to the white-looking screw.

Breaker off, then prove it dead at the outlet. Test both halves, half-switched and multi-wire outlets can have one half live with the other dead.
Standard duplex receptacle, front view, plate removed
Brass screws, right side: Hot (black). One screw per wire.
Silver screws, left side: Neutral (white)
Green ground screw: lower left, bare/green wire

Wide slot (left) = neutral. Narrow slot (right) = hot. Brass right / silver left holds true whether the device is installed ground-up or ground-down. Feeding another outlet? Pigtail the hots together instead of relying on the device to carry the circuit. Break-off tab: snap only to split top/bottom (half-switched).

Step by step
  1. Kill the breaker and verify dead at the device with a tester.
  2. Strip about 3/4", hook each wire, and land it clockwise under its screw: black on brass, white on silver, bare/green on the green screw.
  3. If the outlet feeds another one downstream, pigtail: splice the two blacks plus a short jumper, splice the two whites plus a jumper, and land only the jumpers on the device. That way a bad device doesn't kill the rest of the circuit.
  4. Fold the wires in, seat the device square (a crooked device cracks plates and stresses the yoke), snug the mounting screws without over-tightening.
  5. Power on, then test with a $15 plug-in outlet tester. It confirms polarity and ground in about two seconds.
Slots, polarity, and why it matters
  • Narrow slot = hot. Wide slot = neutral. Round hole = ground. That geometry is what makes polarized plugs work.
  • Reversed polarity (hot on silver, neutral on brass) still lights up a lamp, but the lamp's shell and switch stay energized when it's "off." A tester catches it instantly; fix it by swapping the two wires.
  • Loose neutral is the #1 cause of scorched receptacles. If the terminal is warm, discolored, or the plug falls out on its own, replace the device.
Picking the right device
  • 15 A vs 20 A: a 20 A receptacle has a T-shaped neutral slot and belongs on a 20 A circuit wired with 12 AWG. A standard 15 A receptacle is allowed on a 20 A circuit when there are multiple outlets, but a 20 A device must never go on a 15 A / 14 AWG circuit.
  • Tamper-resistant (TR) receptacles are required in most living areas of dwellings, the shuttered ones. They're the default replacement now.
  • Weather-resistant (WR) plus a proper cover for anything outdoors or in a damp location.
  • GFCI protection for kitchens, baths, laundry, garages, basements, and outdoors, and AFCI for most living spaces. See GFCI, line vs load, for how those get wired.
Decora and half-switched outlets
  • Decora-style receptacles, the flat rectangular face, use the same brass/silver/green terminals as a standard duplex. Different look, identical wiring.
  • Half-hot ("switched") outlets: the small metal break-off tab between the two brass screws is what ties the top and bottom halves together. Snap it out with pliers and you can feed the top half from a switch and the bottom half constant. There's a matching tab on the neutral side for split circuits, leave that one alone unless you know you need it.
Common mistakes
  • Using the backstab holes instead of the screws, they loosen and arc over time.
  • Two wires jammed under one screw instead of a pigtail.
  • Ground left off because "the old one didn't have one."
  • Replacing a two-prong outlet with a three-prong on ungrounded wiring, that's a code violation and it lies to everything you plug in. A GFCI labeled "No Equipment Ground" is the correct fix.

3-way switch wiring

A 3-way switch lets two switches control one light (top and bottom of a staircase, both ends of a hallway). Each switch has a common (COM) terminal and two traveler terminals. Power comes into one common, leaves out the other common to the fixture, and the travelers connect the two switches.

Neutral (white) runs straight from source to fixture. Travelers connect the two switches. The COM terminal is usually a different color (dark/black) from the traveler screws.

  • The travelers are interchangeable, if the light works backwards (off in the middle), you wired a traveler to a common. Swap them.
  • Modern code (2020 NEC) requires a neutral at the switch box for most installs to support smart switches.

GFCI receptacle, LINE vs LOAD

A GFCI (Ground-Fault Circuit Interrupter) compares the current going out the hot to the current coming back on the neutral. If they differ by more than about 5 milliamps, it trips, fast enough to stop a fatal shock. The trick to wiring one is the difference between LINE and LOAD.

Breaker (20A) → GFCI Receptacle → Standard Outlet
Breaker 20A
→
GFCI Receptacle
LINE in / LOAD out
TEST · RESET
→
Standard Outlet
(protected)

⚠ LINE ≠ LOAD. Reverse them and the TEST button won't work, outlet is unprotected.

  • LINE: the wires coming from the breaker (incoming power). Always land these here.
  • LOAD: wires going to downstream outlets you want the GFCI to also protect. Leave it empty if there's nothing downstream.
  • Most common mistake: wiring incoming power on LOAD. The outlet will appear to work but the TEST button does nothing, meaning zero shock protection. Always press TEST after install; the RESET button should pop out and the outlet should go dead.
  • Required by code in: bathrooms, kitchens (counter outlets), garages, unfinished basements, crawlspaces, outdoors, laundry, near sinks, and within 6 ft of any water source.

Knob-and-tube, how old houses were wired

Standard wiring method from about 1880 to 1940. Hot and neutral were run as separate single conductors, supported by ceramic knobs nailed to joists and protected by ceramic tubes wherever they passed through wood framing.

Hot conductor · Neutral conductor · Ceramic tubes

Splices: twisted, soldered, taped, no junction box. No ground wire · Air-cooled (overheats under insulation) · 70+ year old insulation.

  • No ground wire. Every outlet on K&T is ungrounded, a shock hazard for any modern 3-prong appliance.
  • Designed to be air-cooled. The wide spacing and open framing let heat dissipate. Bury it under blown-in insulation and it overheats, a documented fire cause in K&T homes.
  • Splices are open-air: twisted together, soldered, and wrapped in cloth tape. Modern code requires every splice in a junction box.
  • Insurance: many carriers won't cover a home with active K&T circuits, see the Inspection Helper for what replacement typically costs.
🧵

Cloth (rag) wiring

Used from roughly the 1920s through the mid-1960s as the transition between knob-and-tube and modern plastic-jacketed NM (Romex). Two or three conductors with rubber insulation, bundled inside a woven cloth outer jacket.

Cloth-jacketed cable (pre-1965), cross-section

Brittle rubber insulation inside woven cloth jacket. Often no ground wire (2-wire only) · Rubber crumbles when disturbed · Cloth dries out, exposing conductor.

  • Often no ground. Early cloth cable was 2-wire only; a ground conductor wasn't added until the late 1950s.
  • Brittle rubber. The rubber insulation under the cloth jacket dries out and crumbles when disturbed. Any future work on the circuit cracks more insulation off, exposing bare conductor inside the wall.
  • The cloth itself frays near devices and panel entries, a common reason old houses have exposed copper inside boxes.
  • Replacing whole-house cloth wiring is a major project; partial rewires (worst circuits first) are sometimes a workable phase-one approach.
📋

Common code minimums homeowners ask about

  • Outlet spacing (12-ft rule): in habitable rooms, no point along a wall should be more than 6 ft from a receptacle (so outlets every 12 ft minimum).
  • Tamper-resistant outlets: required in dwellings since 2008, the little shutters that block kids from poking things into the slots.
  • GFCI: bath (since 1975), kitchen counters (since 1987), garages, basements, crawlspaces, outdoors, laundry, anywhere within 6 ft of water.
  • AFCI: required on most living-area circuits (bedrooms since 1999, expanded to most rooms in 2014).
  • Working clearance at the panel: 30" wide × 36" deep × 6'-6" tall of clear space in front of any panel. Don't stack the laundry in front of it.
  • Permits: almost every panel swap, service upgrade, and new circuit requires a permit and inspection. Skipping it can void insurance and complicate a home sale.
